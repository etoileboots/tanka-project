About this Project

About translation: Translation matters. When reading any work of translation, the translator's interpretation becomes intrinsically tied with, and affects, the original content. In translation, things such as structure, tone, and even content vary from the original work.

About motivation: This project was inspired by a curiosity for how we can better support understanding and visualizing comparative translations (the study of contrasting translations of the same work in terms of strategy and outcome, side by side). It focuses on how visualization specifically can be used to encourage this understanding.

About the project: This project is a digital collection of the Ogura Hyakunin Isshu (OHI), a 13th-century Japanese poetry anthology containing 100 poems from 100 poets, along with four English translations of these poems. The OHI has been translated over a dozen times. The choice of translators to include in this analysis was determined by data availability and access; only translations within the public domain were included. The translators and their respective publication years are: Frederick Victor Dickens (1865/1866), Yone Noguchi (1907), Clay MacCauley (1899/1917), and William Ninnis Porter (1909).

When zooming into a poem (by the up arrow key), you'll see these names abbreviated to their initials: O (original), D (Dickens), N (Noguchi), M (MacCauley), and P (Porter). More detail on the source material itself is in About waka poetry and the OHI.

The visualizations in this analysis are largely inspired by the diagrams used in syntenic analysis. Synteny is a genomics concept used to study the physical arrangement of DNA sequences and gene positions across species. It is a powerful tool for studying the relationship between ancestor and descendant species, measuring evolutionary distance, and identifying mutations and inversions. More on synteny and how I've adapted it here is in About synteny.

About waka poetry and the OHI: Waka poetry is a classical form of Japanese poetry that follows a 31-syllable structure arranged into a 5-7-5-7-7 meter across five ku (verses). You'll notice the first three ku form a haiku.

The OHI translates to "Ogura's one hundred people, one hundred poems," and contains, as the name suggests, 100 poems from 100 poets. The most well-known version was collected by the anthologist Fujiwara no Teika for his home near Ogura Mountain, in Kyoto.

Waka poetry is distinct for a few reasons.

First, it's divided into an upper and lower section. The first three ku form the upper part, called the kami-no-ku (5-7-5), which typically sets the scene through physical descriptions of nature. The last two ku form the lower part, called the shimo-no-ku (7-7), which often shifts to the author's internal reflection. Within these sections — often overlapping — is a pivot that connects observation with revelation. Translations may invert this narrative structure, or add extra detail to connect the two sections.

Second, waka poetry employs various literary devices. These aren't found in every waka poem, but they're common and relatively standard. This project focuses on drawing out three of them:

掛詞 Kakekotoba — a "pivot word": a single phonetic word carrying two meanings at once, often bridging the kami-no-ku and the shimo-no-ku.
枕詞 Makurakotoba — literally "pillow word": a fixed 5-syllable epithet placed before specific words (e.g. god, mountain) to add rhythm and evoke imagery.
季語 Kigo — a "season word": a word associated with a specific season (e.g. cherry blossoms for spring) that signals the time of year without necessarily stating the season directly-though sometimes it does.

There are several other literary techniques used in waka poetry, but these are the three devices this analysis focuses on.

About synteny: A brief introduction to synteny is in About project; this section explains synteny's use for in visualization for comparative translation, and explains the parallel I'm drawing between two seemingly disparate fields.

Synteny is useful for spotting evolutionary shifts. If two species originate from one ancestor species, they'll have copied genes from that ancestor, and share genes between themselves. This copying process is inevitably subject to mutations such as deletions, translocations, duplications, or inversions. Synteny is a way to study these changes: if genetic information and order are perfectly conserved, that's perfect synteny; any of the disruptions above is disrupted synteny. There's both macro- and micro-synteny. Macrosynteny looks at whether large blocks of genes remain on the same chromosome across species. Microsynteny is the conservation of local gene content..

Stated broadly: synteny helps us understand, from an origin to a destination string of information, where information and order was preserved, where it wasn't, and how it was modified.

In comparative translation, we can liken the original work to the "ancestor gene," and the translations to descendants that draw information from the ancestor but rearrange and modify it to reflect their own language and context. The preservation and modification of original information and structure across different translations can be viewed through the lens of synteny.

This analogy works particularly well for waka poetry, which has a clear structure and a defined set of literary devices. Macrosynteny can be viewed as the conservation of the overall structure (kami-no-ku and shimo-no-ku) — taking the content of each and seeing where meaning was preserved, inverted, deleted, or mutated past recognition (resulting in an "imagined line"). Microsynteny looks at the literary devices, to understand the preservation of local content like the kigo and kakekotoba.

Through this view, we can compare not only the original text to a translation, but translations against each other — spotting patterns across translators and poems to ask questions like:

"What was the translator's goal in this poem — to preserve the literal translation, to provide a creative reinterpretation, or something else entirely?"

"What in the source text is easier or more difficult to translate, as seen through patterns of inheritance?"

"How do the origin language and the target language differ in narrative structure — does one allow the structure to be preserved, or does it require shifts?"

About Data Process: For ease of understanding, a simple flowchart of the full data pipeline is included below.

flowchart TD
    A["Raw CSV export"] --> B["ingest_csv.py"]
    B --> C["poems_clean.csv\n100 poems, poet + JP + 4 translations"]
    C --> D["annotate_translations.py — Pass 1\nClaude identifies source devices\n(kakekotoba, makurakotoba, kigo, structure)"]
    D --> E["data/annotations/source/"]
    E --> F["annotate_translations.py — Pass 2\nClaude evaluates each translation\n× 4 translators"]
    F --> G["data/annotations/translations/"]
    G --> H{"review_app — human review"}
    H -->|confirm / remove / correct / add| I["data/corrections/"]
    H -->|no changes needed| J["build_data.py"]
    I --> J
    C -.-> J
    E -.-> J
    G -.-> J
    J --> K["site/data/poems.json"]
    K --> L["site/ — the visualization"]

Corrections don't trigger an automatic rebuild — build_data.py is re-run manually after any annotation or correction batch.

About AI: I used Claude in two ways during this project. First, on the Desktop app, as an engineering tool to build the data pipeline, website, and interactivity. Second, through its API, to run a targeted LLM prompt as a first pass for identifying the structure and literary devices of the original Japanese poem, and their preservation into each translation. You can read the exact prompt used. The design of the website, and of the data pipeline itself, were mine.

This project is not meant to be AI-native, nor to offer a black-box solution to comparative translation as a whole. An expert Japanese/English translator trained in 13th-century waka poetry would classify these translations far more reliably than AI can. Finding and paying such an expert for a personal project, however, isn't realistic.

All AI classifications were spot-checked thoroughly — by me, and by a trained Japanese/English translator who isn't a waka expert: my mom. She was compensated in freshly baked goods and (I hope) the joy of quality time with her daughter, reviewing 500 poems together.
